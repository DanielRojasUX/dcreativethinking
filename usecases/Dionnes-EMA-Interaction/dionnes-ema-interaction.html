Case Study EMA Interaction Design

Sealed on her phone. Zero paid events missed the kitchen.

Role : Head of AI Digital Growth, Branding and Systems
Org : Dionne's Catering
Product : Event Management App (EMA), interface and interaction design
AI partner : Claude
Abstract

The Event Management App already had its model: one record per event. It needed an interface that three very different people would trust on a phone. I built it around the object catering already runs on, the event ticket. Sales pulls each new lead out like paper, the client reviews her contract on the web, unfolds it section by section and seals it with her deposit, and the kitchen watches the paid event land on its board. Dionne approved the design, and the system's result holds: no paid event has gone missing from the production calendar.

01 / Hybrid System

Hybrid
System

  • One system, two doorsThe team installs EMA because they live in it all day. The client never installs anything: a link by text opens her contract in the browser, because nobody downloads an app for two steps.
  • Maya, sales, in the appShe checks in several times a day with one question: is there anything new? New leads lead the screen; leads she is following up sit quietly below.
  • Valeria, the client, on the webShe opens her contract from a text, reviews every detail, messages Maya if something is off, and seals it with her deposit.
  • Luis, the kitchen, in the appThe push arrives the moment the deposit lands, and the event moves to confirmed on his board without anyone retyping it.
  • Who they areThe research behind these three people, and the event model they share, is in the first case study: Dionne's Event Management App.
02 / Visual Language

Visual
Language

  • The ticketEvery event is a ticket: a stub with the date in big numbers, perforations between sections and notches at the main tears. The same object is the client's contract, the kitchen's card and the entry in the calendar.
  • Paper, not panelsRows and sheets carry a paper grain and long fibers, and they behave like paper: they are dragged, folded and stamped.
  • Color by roleEach persona owns a background: charcoal for Maya in sales, magenta, Dionne's brand color, for the client on the web, and deep teal for the kitchen. Accents follow the same split: magenta front of house, mint in the kitchen. Labels always carry the state in words, so color is never the only signal.
  • Type and depthDM Sans runs the interface with numbers at its heaviest weight; Libre Baskerville, upright, names people and events. Nested content folds open one shade darker per level.
03 / Interactions

Three
Interactions

  • Slide inA lead is pulled out like paper. Its detail sheet comes in glued to the row, rigid, following the finger one to one, with nothing animating on its own. New leads come in from the left because they have not happened yet; existing events come in from the right.
  • AccordionSections unfold from their perforations in 3D. The shade darkens as the paper bends, the page color shows behind each crease, and edges toward the fold stay square.
  • The stampPaying is sealing. The client slides to seal, the stamp lands on the stub, and her status turns confirmed in the same color as the page around her ticket.
  • Motion specEntrances ease out on cubic bezier (0.22, 1, 0.36, 1) and exits are shorter and ease in. Nothing is linear, and every transition ends within 700 ms. Everything else on screen stays still.
04 / Accessibility

Accessi­bility

  • Contrast, measuredEvery text pair passes WCAG 2.2 AA. The muted gray was lifted until it passed on the darkest card, and the kitchen's mint carries charcoal text instead of white.
  • A tap for every gestureWCAG 2.2 asks for a single pointer alternative to dragging (2.5.7). Rows open with a tap or their chevron, and the seal arms on the first tap and seals on the second.
  • States beyond colorWaiting cards are solid sand with a dark label; confirmed cards are paper with a colored label. Stripes stay only where no text sits on them.
  • Keyboard and motionEvery control works by keyboard, and under reduced motion folds and slides resolve at once.

What went well?

One object, three peopleThe ticket the client seals is the card the kitchen receives, so nobody has to translate between screens.

Paper that behaves like paperRigid drags, square folds and a stamp make the interface feel physical and predictable.

Accessibility before the final screensContrast and tap alternatives were fixed while designing, not after review.

AI as a builder, not the designerClaude produced and checked the prototypes; every pattern, correction and rule was mine.
