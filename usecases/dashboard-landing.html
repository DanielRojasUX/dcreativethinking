Kyndryl Bridge

Dashboard Landing

Transforming a static enterprise link farm into a personalized operating hub.

Sr Lead UX Designer · IBM / Kyndryl
5+ Independent Apps 140K+ Users 1-Year Multi-Team Build CEO-Approved Reallocation Design Ops Governance
The apparent problem

Nobody used the landing page

The existing landing page functioned as a static link farm meant to help new users find their way. Actual engagement was under 5%. Most regular users bypassed it entirely, bookmarking the specific tools they needed directly in their browser.

The complexity

But the ask was to make it prettier

The business asked for a one-month cosmetic refresh. Kyndryl Bridge is a modular multi-cloud management platform where five or more applications operate independently but share common accounts, identity, and permissions underneath. A visual reskin couldn't touch that structural mismatch, and it wouldn't survive contact with how the platform actually worked.

The evidence

What the research actually showed

I interviewed application product owners, delivery partners, and development managers, and analyzed real usage data. The pattern that surfaced: users were completely unaware of new features shipping inside individual applications, because their daily workflows never touched anything outside their own bookmarked corner of the platform. Multi-million dollar capabilities were shipping into a void.

The insight

A prettier front door wasn't the fix

A static transitional page is useless to a daily enterprise operator. People didn't need a nicer entry point, they needed an operating hub that brought their actual work to them, personalized to their role and permissions from the moment they logged in.

The critical decision

I rejected the one-month cosmetic brief and pitched a complete strategic pivot to the CEO instead.

Armed with usage data and low-fidelity interactive prototypes, I proposed reallocating engineering resources away from a cosmetic refresh and into a year-long platform overhaul: turn the static landing page into a dynamic, widget-driven operating surface. It was approved.

The system, built

I designed pre-built dashboard templates tied to specific persona workflows, so the blank-canvas onboarding problem never happened, people saw immediate, contextual value the moment they logged in. Drag-and-drop widget placement let each user shape the hub around their own daily work.

The system, in depth

The parts above are enough to see what shipped. Open any of these for how it actually works underneath.

To keep widgets from turning into bloated micro-applications, I set a single rule: a widget has to let someone check status and trigger one primary action with one hand, a single click. The moment a task needed a second hand, typing, a form, deeper data entry, the widget routed straight into the full application instead of trying to hold that complexity itself.

Governing five or more independent application teams needed more than a style guide. I established a two-tier clearance process run through Azure DevOps: a design certification step, where app teams submitted widgets for a five-minute review against five non-negotiable points, no text inputs, real-estate usage, grid alignment, typography scale, cognitive load, followed by an engineering handoff that only released for build once a ticket carried an explicit design approval, and a final visual QA pass before it shipped.

Lowering the friction with modular card guidelines got application teams to actually adopt the catalog. It launched with around 60 widgets and organically grew to more than 150. The unexpected part: lightweight widgets became low-risk test beds, teams watched how people used them on the shared dashboard and used that signal to justify entirely new, full-scale application features.

What Went Well?

Effective Leadership Pitch: The low-fidelity mockups and strategic presentation successfully secured leadership buy-in, transforming the scope from a static UI update to a dynamic, user-centric dashboard project.

Data-Driven Decisions: Insights from user interviews and subject-matter experts shaped the dashboard's personalized features, directly addressing low engagement (5% usage) and improving relevance for daily workflows.

Improved Engagement and Efficiency: Post-launch, the dashboard saw a 20% increase in daily usage, with task completion times reduced by 35% due to streamlined navigation and customizable widgets.

Collaborative Alignment: Established clear widget guidelines and streamlined multi-team collaboration, ensuring consistent standards across a diverse platform architecture.

What Could I Have Done Better?

Facilitating Team Engagement: While I successfully led the internal playbacks and ensured alignment, I could have invited team members to co-facilitate certain sessions. This approach would have encouraged broader ownership, nurtured cross-functional leadership, and fostered a more diverse exchange of ideas.

Optimizing Testing Scope: Although the user testing yielded actionable insights, expanding the scope to include a wider variety of edge cases could have further validated the dashboard's versatility and uncovered additional opportunities for refinement.

Structured Post-Launch Monitoring: While the iterative improvement process was effective, establishing a more formalized post-launch monitoring framework could have streamlined the collection of user feedback, enabling quicker adjustments and long-term optimization.
